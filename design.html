First — what are we even making?

The one-sentence version

why this project exists at all
?

You want an AI to look at a clothing product — a title and description scraped from a shop — and fill in a form about it. Sleeve length, colour, neckline, what occasion it suits. Thousands of products. Automatically.

To train an AI to do that, you need something that can mark its homework, instantly, thousands of times a second. Not a human. A program.

That marking program is the entire project. Everything else — the AI, the training, the GPU bill — is downstream of it. If the marker is wrong, you train the AI to be confidently wrong, and you won't find out for weeks.

The clever bit in your plan: the same marker gets used twice. Once as the thing that scores the AI during training, and once as the quality gate in the real product. Build it once, two jobs. That's the "one verifier, two consumers" line.

Step 1 · The list of allowed answers

Turning the form into dropdown menus

commit dd8ba2c · packs/vastraa_taste_v1/vocab.yaml
1

Why a fixed list, not free typing

If the AI can write anything it wants, you can never mark it. Is "quite long" the same as "long"? Is "midnight" the same as "navy"? You'd be arguing with a computer forever.

So the form has no text boxes — only dropdown menus. Every question has a fixed set of answers. Now marking is just "is this one of the allowed options, yes or no."
sleeve_length:  sleeveless | short | elbow | three_quarter | long
neckline:       crew | scoop | v_neck | square | boat | halter | ...
fit:            tight | regular | loose | oversized
2

We borrowed the list instead of inventing it

There's a free, expert-built list of clothing attributes called Fashionpedia — made by fashion researchers, published at a proper conference. It has 294 attributes.

Why borrow rather than invent? Because later, when the AI says "long sleeve" and your answer key says "3/4 sleeve", you need to know who's wrong. If you invented the categories, maybe your categories were just bad. Borrowing an expert's list takes that excuse off the table.

It's the difference between "I built a tagger" and "I built a tagger that measures against a published standard." Only one of those survives someone poking at it.
3

Then we cut 294 down to 15

You do not want all 294. Every extra question means 300 more things you have to check by hand later, and one more place the AI can cheat. So each candidate had to survive six filters:

FilterMeaning
Fixed options?Must be a real dropdown, not open-ended.
In the words?We're reading product text, not photos. If a shop never types it, it's useless to us.
Fast to decide?If you would hesitate 10 seconds, cut it — you're doing 300 of these by hand.
Common enough?An option that appears 3 times out of 300 makes the score wobble randomly.
Has dependencies?We want some questions that only apply sometimes — that's what the rules check.
Actually useful?Does Vastraa's "what does this person wear" feature need it?

Landed on 15 — the bare minimum your plan allowed. Adding more later is easy. Un-labelling 300 items is not.

4

Three surprises from reading the actual data

Rather than trusting memory, we downloaded the real Fashionpedia file. Three assumptions died:

1. It has no fabric attributes. At all. No cotton, no silk, no wool. It covers plastic, metal, gem, fur — and that's it. So cotton is honestly labelled "we made this up, here's why" rather than pretending it came from the experts.

2. Two of its categories are secretly two things each. Its "length" bucket mixes hem length and sleeve length in one pile. Its "silhouette" bucket mixes shape with fit. We split both.

3. "Sleeveless" looks rare and isn't. It shows up 12 times — but only because sleeveless clothes have no sleeve to annotate. That's absence, not rarity. Deleting it for being rare would have been a real mistake.

This is why "go read the data" beat "write what sounds right." A file claiming cotton came from Fashionpedia would have looked completely fine — and been false.
5

Every answer says where it came from

Next to each option is a tag: direct (copied from the experts), derived (we merged a few of theirs, here's which), or custom (we invented it, here's why).

- name: crew
  aliases: [crew neck, crewneck, round neck, jewel neck]
  from:
    type: derived
    op: merge
    fp:
      - { k: attribute, id: 181, name: "crew (neck)" }
      - { k: attribute, id: 182, name: "round (neck)" }
    note: "shops don't distinguish these two"

And there's a program that checks those tags — it looks up every ID number in the real Fashionpedia file and fails if the name doesn't match exactly.

This turns "everything is traceable" from something you say into something a computer confirms. Big difference when someone asks.
To make sure the checker wasn't just rubber-stamping everything, we broke the file five ways on purpose — wrong ID, misspelled name, missing justification, duplicate nickname, phantom category. It caught all five.
6

Nicknames

Shops write the same thing a dozen ways: 3/4, three-quarter, elbow length, 3/4 slv. So every option carries a nickname list — 754 of them across the pack. That's the difference between working on a demo and working on a real shop feed.

Step 2 · The marker

The program that grades the AI's homework

commit dd280c6 · verifier/
1

It asks three questions, separately

The AI hands in something. The marker asks:

QuestionExample failure
1Is this even a filled-in form? Right boxes, right shapes.It wrote a paragraph instead of a form
2Did it stay in the dropdowns?sleeve_length: "quite long"
3Does it contradict itself?"sleeveless" and "puff sleeves"
from verifier import load_pack, verify

pack   = load_pack("packs/vastraa_taste_v1")
result = verify(model_output, pack)

result.schema_valid      # question 1
result.vocab_valid       # question 2
result.rule_violations   # question 3 — which rules broke
2

Why three separate scores and not one

This is the most important design decision in the whole step, so here it is slowly.

Next week you start paying the AI for good answers. If you only ever tell it "wrong", it has no idea which part it got wrong.

It's a teacher handing back a paper with a single ✗ on the front and nothing else. Technically accurate. Completely useless for learning.

Split into three, you can watch each skill improve at its own speed. In practice the AI nails "produce valid JSON" quickly, then spends ages learning "stop contradicting yourself."

That graph is the interesting result. It's what the blog post is about. Mashing the three into one number erases it permanently — and you'd never know it was gone.

Practically this meant building two validators instead of one: a loose one that only checks the shape, and a strict one that checks the values. The obvious shortcut — one validator that does both — is less code and quietly destroys a measurement you can't get back.

3

The rules, in plain YAML

Contradiction-checking is written as data, not code, so you can add rules without touching the program. Three kinds cover everything so far:

# if it's a dress, it must have a length and a neckline
- id: dress_needs_length_and_neckline
  type: requires
  if: { garment_category: dress }
  then_present: [garment_length, neckline]

# sleeveless things can't have a sleeve style
- id: sleeveless_has_no_sleeve_style
  type: excludes
  if: { sleeve_length: sleeveless }
  forbid: { sleeve_style: "*" }

# trousers can't be "mini" length
- id: pants_length_subset
  type: conditional_vocab
  if: { garment_category: pants }
  restrict: { garment_length: [cropped, knee, midi, maxi] }

25 written by hand. Then 9 more generated automatically from the vocabulary file — because the vocab already knows that "neckline" doesn't apply to shoes. Copying that knowledge into a second file by hand would just mean two copies that slowly disagree.

34 rules for the effort of writing 25.
4

The spacecraft pack

Your plan insists the marker must never know it's dealing with clothes — so that pack #2 can be a completely different shop's schema with zero code changes. Easy to claim. Very easy to accidentally break with one stray if garment_category ==.

So there's a fake schema about spacecraft — mission class, propulsion, hull material. Three invented fields, two rules, two YAML files, zero lines of code. Then every test runs twice: once on clothes, once on spacecraft.

If anything clothing-specific sneaks into the marker, the spacecraft run fails and you find out in one second instead of in week four.

It worked immediately — it caught the Step 1 checker hard-coding garment_category. The claim is now enforced by a machine instead of trusted.

There's even a test asserting a pack folder contains no .py files at all. If a pack ever needs code, it isn't a pack any more.

5

"I don't know" vs "doesn't apply"

Two different kinds of blank, and they must never be confused:

ValueMeansExample
nullQuestion doesn't apply to this itemneckline, on a handbag
"unknown"The AI chose not to answerdescription was too vague

Why it matters: in week 4 you pay +1 for right, 0 for "I don't know", −λ for wrong. Deliberately declining is worth more than guessing badly — that's the entire experiment. If both blanks looked identical, you couldn't count them, and the experiment wouldn't run.

There's a knob per rule, allow_abstain. Left open, an AI could dodge every rule by answering "unknown" to everything. Three rules currently refuse it. Worth a look before you freeze the answer key.
6

The marker never tidies up before marking

AIs love wrapping answers in markdown fences, adding trailing commas, chatting before the JSON. It's tempting to strip all that automatically.

But that's fixing the student's spelling before you mark the spelling test. Next week's whole point is watching the AI learn to produce clean output. Quietly repairing it deletes that lesson before it's ever measured.

So the marker is strict by default. Nickname-matching exists but is opt-in, and it reports exactly what it changed — because "how often was the AI one nickname away from correct" is itself a useful number.

7

The bug the tests caught

A test fed the marker deliberate garbage. The marker crashed.

That's worse than an ordinary bug. This thing's whole job is grading garbage — during training the AI absolutely will emit nonsense, that's the point of running it unconstrained. A marker that dies on nonsense takes the training run down with it, and you lose an evening working out why.

Specifically: a list where a single value belonged made it throw instead of scoring. Fixed — and there's now a test firing eight kinds of junk at it, because "never crash" is a property worth defending permanently.

Then Step 3

the week-eater — timebox it
1

300 frozen + 3–5k cheap

A small answer key you correct by hand and never train on. A big rough set you don't fully trust and never test on. Keeping those two apart is the thing that stops every number afterwards from being a lie.

2

The marker is already waiting for it

verify_record() takes a plain dictionary, so labelled rows can be checked the moment they exist — before any AI is involved. If your own hand-labels break the rules, the rules are wrong, and better to learn that at row 20 than row 300.

Order matters: the list defines what "correct" means, the marker makes it checkable, and everything after — the training runs, the frontier baseline, the abstention experiment — is measured with these two tools. Which is why both of them are themselves tested rather than trusted: a quiet mistake here doesn't crash anything, it just makes five weeks of numbers meaningless.
